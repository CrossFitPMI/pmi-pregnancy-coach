01 · The coach’s job

Not fragile, not invincible

You don’t need to have been pregnant to coach a pregnant athlete. You need to understand the changes happening, coach the athlete in front of you, recognise when a movement needs adapting, and know when something is outside your scope.

Pregnancy doesn’t automatically mean “stop CrossFit”. The goal is to keep her training where appropriate, adapting the stimulus, environment, movement and intensity as her needs change. CrossFit’s guidance puts mechanics and consistency before intensity, with individual and quick scaling when movement quality or symptoms change.

Our gym approach

  • Don’t panic. Pregnancy isn’t automatically a reason to remove an athlete from class.
  • Don’t ignore it. Her changing body and symptoms need active coaching.
  • No one-size-fits-all trimester chart. Two athletes at the same stage can need very different things.
  • Preserve the training goal. Change the movement if needed, but keep the intended stimulus.
  • Coach the athlete, not her pregnancy week. Ask, observe, adjust, reassess.
The key question isn’t “Can pregnant women do this movement?” It’s “Can this athlete do this movement today with good mechanics, breathing, comfort and risk management?”
02 · When she tells you

Calm, private & supportive

Congratulate her, ask how she’s feeling and find out what support she wants. Never announce it or discuss her pregnancy with other members.

Useful questions

  • How far along are you?
  • Have you talked to your maternity-care provider about continuing to exercise?
  • Were you training before pregnancy, and what was your normal training load?
  • Have you been given any medical restrictions or specific instructions?
  • Are there movements you already know feel uncomfortable?
  • Are you having any leaking, pelvic heaviness or pressure, pain, dizziness or unusual symptoms during training?
Don’t interrogate her or ask for private medical details you don’t need. Our job is to coach movement and training. Clinical decisions belong with her healthcare professionals.

Internal gym process

Nominate one lead coach for continuity. Make sure every coach who might coach her knows her agreed scaling strategy. Keep it confidential and professional.

03 · The five-step process

Every class, every movement

  1. Check inHow are you feeling today? Any symptoms or changes since last session?
  2. ObserveWatch mechanics, breathing, balance, range, pressure management and comfort.
  3. Identify the problemIs it impact, balance, abdominal space, pressure, fatigue, range, speed, load or complexity?
  4. Preserve the stimulusChoose a simpler movement that trains the same physical quality.
  5. ReassessDoes the new version feel better and look controlled? If not, scale again.
Scaling isn’t failure. A good scale is successful coaching. Reduce load, slow the pace, add breaks or reduce complexity as needed.

05 · Trimester guidance

Context, not a prescription

Many athletes can continue familiar training. Symptoms may be the biggest driver: nausea, fatigue, dizziness, heat intolerance, altered recovery or lower tolerance for intensity. Have alternatives ready without assuming every movement must change.

As the abdomen grows, movement space, balance, centre of mass and comfort can change. Start adapting setup, range, impact, floor transitions and bar path as needed. Don’t wait until a movement becomes obviously impossible.

The emphasis often shifts further toward stable, controlled and sustainable training. More rest, lower loads, shorter ranges, supported positions and other conditioning options are excellent training, not “doing nothing”.

One athlete may change a movement in the first trimester, another may keep it much longer, and another may never need that modification. We respond to the athlete.
06 · Intensity, breathing &amp; fatigue

No universal heart-rate ceiling

Don’t coach pregnancy by an old universal heart-rate limit. ACOG notes heart-rate responses change in pregnancy, and perceived exertion and the talk test are useful ways to monitor intensity. For a previously active athlete with an uncomplicated pregnancy, higher intensity may still be appropriate when tolerated and medically appropriate.

Coach for

  • An appropriate effort level and breathing for her
  • Enough rest between efforts
  • Good mechanics under fatigue
  • A controlled environment and hydration
  • No pressure to match pre-pregnancy scores
If she can’t maintain the movement because she’s exhausted, breathless, unstable or losing control, reduce the demand before adding more fatigue.

Longer or harder sessions increase fuelling and hydration needs. Never encourage restriction or weight-loss dieting during pregnancy.

07 · Symptoms

Green, yellow, red

Green: coach & monitor

Normal day-to-day variation, manageable fatigue, a movement becoming awkward, reduced range, needing more rest, or a movement just feeling less comfortable. These usually call for a coaching adjustment.

Yellow: modify or stop that movement

Pain, pelvic pressure or heaviness, leaking, significant or repeated abdominal doming/coning, loss of control, instability, or a movement that consistently feels wrong. Stop the movement, modify and reassess. Persistent symptoms need a clinical referral.

Red: stop exercise & refer

ACOG warning signs that mean she stops exercising and seeks medical advice:

  • Vaginal bleeding
  • Fluid leakage or a gush from the vagina
  • Regular painful contractions
  • Dizziness or fainting
  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath before exercise
  • Severe or unusual headache
  • Muscle weakness affecting balance
  • Calf pain or swelling
Don’t diagnose. Don’t tell her a symptom is “normal pregnancy”. Stop, note it appropriately and direct her to her maternity-care provider when needed.
08 · The gym environment

Coach the room, not just the athlete

Pregnancy changes balance and centre of mass, and CrossFit guidance specifically highlights avoiding impact to the abdomen from falls, equipment or unstable situations.

  • Keep lanes clear.
  • Where practical, avoid placing her beside high-traffic lifting or jumping areas.
  • Be especially cautious with rope climbs, box jumps, handstands and other movements with real fall risk as pregnancy progresses.
  • Give her enough space to set up and move safely.
  • Keep loads and implements at a weight she can easily control.
  • Don’t single her out when scaling. Normalise modifications for everyone.
A great coach makes the modification look like part of the programme, not a public announcement about her pregnancy.
09 · Core, supine &amp; pressure

Train it, don’t fear it

Avoid turning “core” into a fear-based category. We’re looking for her to manage breathing, trunk pressure and movement control. Coning is useful feedback but not a diagnosis by itself. Use it as a cue to reduce the demand or change position, range, speed or load, then reassess.

Supine work

ACOG advises avoiding long periods lying flat on the back as pregnancy progresses, especially after around 20 weeks, because the uterus can reduce venous return. Use an incline, side-lying, seated, standing or other comfortable option.

Good options: Pallof presses, carries, supported rows, standing knee raises, side-lying work and controlled breathing and brace patterns. The aim isn’t to remove trunk training. It’s to train it appropriately.

10 · Real class examples

What it looks like on the floor

WOD: 400m run + 20 box jumps

Athlete says running feels heavy and landings feel uncomfortable.

→ Run → bike, row or walk. Box jumps → controlled step-ups. Keep the work interval and lower-body conditioning.
WOD: 21-15-9 thrusters + burpees

Bump is getting in the way of the bar path and burpees feel crowded.

→ DB or goblet squat-to-press and elevated step-back burpees. Keeps squat, press and conditioning.
Strength: 5 × 5 deadlift

Bar is contacting the abdomen and the start position feels uncomfortable.

→ Raise the bar, use DBs or a wider stance. Lower the load if mechanics or pressure management change.
Gymnastics: pull-ups + toes-to-bar

Kipping is uncomfortable and there’s abdominal coning.

→ Ring rows or banded pulls, plus standing knee raises or Pallof press. Don’t chase the original reps if quality drops.
“I feel fine”, but the movement has changed

You see an unstable overhead position and loss of control.

→ Trust what you observe. Reduce load or complexity, change the movement, or end that movement if needed.
11 · How we talk to her

Language matters

“You can’t do that because you’re pregnant.”
“Let’s see how that feels today and adjust if needed.”
“You should probably stop lifting heavy.”
“Let’s change the setup so you have more room.”
“That’s dangerous for the baby.”
“I want to keep the stimulus but make the movement more comfortable.”
“Pregnant women shouldn’t do CrossFit.”
“How does your pelvic floor / abdomen feel with that?”
Or try this:
“Let’s reduce the intensity and see if your mechanics improve.”
Don’t assume what she wants. Some women want to train hard and adapt; others want a lower-stress pregnancy. Our job is to support her goals within appropriate coaching and medical boundaries.
